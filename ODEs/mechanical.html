21. Mechanical Systems#

We consider a system of point masses \(m_i\) at time-dependent positions \(x_i(t)\).

There is potential energy \(U(x)\) stored in that system. Examples are gravitational energy

\[ \sum m_i g x_{i,z}, \]

or energy stored in springs \(k_{ij}\) connecting masses \(m_i\) and \(m_j\):

\[ \sum_{k_{ij}} \frac{1}{2} k_{ij} ( |x_i - x_j | - l_{ij} )^2, \]

where \(l_{ij}\) is the length of the spring without load, and \(|x_i - x_j|\) is the Euclidean distance.

Taking the negative derivative

\[ F_i := -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i}(x) \]

gives the force acting on the mass point \(i\).

In case of gravity it is \(-m_i g e_z\), in case of springs it is

\[ -\sum_j k_{ij} ( |x_i - x_j | - l_{ij} ) \frac{x_i - x_j}{|x_i - x_j|} \]

i.e. spring constant multiplied by elongation, in the direction of the vector \(x_j-x_i\). The sum runs over all mass points \(j\) connected to mass point \(i\) with a spring.

By Newton’s principle, the force leads to acceleration:

\[ m_i \ddot x_i = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i} \]

Here, \(\dot {x}\) denotes the derivative w.r.t. time \(t\).

21.1. Energy conservation#

The total energy is the sum of kinetic and potential energy:

\[ E(t) = \sum_i \frac{1}{2} m_i |\dot x_i(t)|^2 + U(x(t)). \]

Along a solution it is constant:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} E = \sum_i m_i \, \dot x_i \cdot \ddot x_i + \sum_i \frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i} \cdot \dot x_i = \sum_i \Big( m_i \ddot x_i + \frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i} \Big) \cdot \dot x_i = 0. \]

For simulations over long times, a numerical method should not destroy this property. We have seen in section Stability for linear ODEs for the oscillator \(y^{\prime \prime} = -\omega^2 y\) that the explicit Euler method increases the energy, the implicit Euler method damps it, and the Crank-Nicolson method preserves it exactly. For non-linear problems, so called symplectic methods (for example the implicit mid-point rule, Gauss-Legendre methods, and the Störmer-Verlet method below) do not preserve the energy exactly. But the energy error stays bounded by \(O(\tau^p)\) over very long times, there is no drift.

21.2. The Newmark method#

We start with reducing the second order equation to a first order system. Therefore, we introduce velocity \(v_i = \dot x_i\):

\[\begin{split} \begin{array}{rcl} \dot x_i & = & v_i \\ m_i \dot v_i & = & F_i(x) \end{array} \end{split}\]

Applying the Crank-Nicolson time-stepping scheme we obtain

\[\begin{split} \begin{array}{rcl} x_{n+1} - x_n & = & \frac{\tau}{2} (v_n + v_{n+1} ) \\ M (v_{n+1} - v_n) & = & \frac{\tau}{2} (F(x_n) + F(x_{n+1})) \end{array} \end{split}\]

By renaming \(a_n := M^{-1} F(x_n)\), we can substitute

\[\begin{split} \begin{array}{rcl} v_{n+1} & = & v_n + \frac{\tau}{2} (a_n + a_{n+1}) \\ x_{n+1} & = & x_n + \frac{\tau}{2} (v_n + v_{n+1}) = x_n + \tau v_n + \frac{\tau^2}{4} (a_n + a_{n+1} ) \end{array} \end{split}\]

and obtain the equation for the new \(a_{n+1}\):

\[ M a_{n+1} = F \big( x_n + \tau v_n + \tfrac{\tau^2}{4} (a_n + a_{n+1} ) \big) \]

The dimension of the nonlinear system is the same as the number of state variables (and not twice the size, as it looks after introducing the first order system).

The function SolveODE_Newmark in mechsystem/Newmark.hpp (branch Newmark) solves \(M \ddot x = F(x)\). Both \(M\) and \(F\) are given as NonlinearFunctions. The new acceleration is the independent variable, and the function algebra expresses the new velocity and position as functions of it (with \(\gamma = \frac{1}{2}\), \(\beta = \frac{1}{4}\)):

auto anew = std::make_shared<IdentityFunction>(a.size());
auto vnew = vold + dt*((1-gamma)*aold+gamma*anew);
auto xnew = xold + dt*vold + dt*dt/2 * ((1-2*beta)*aold+2*beta*anew);

auto equ = Compose(mass, anew) - Compose(rhs, xnew);

In every time-step, Newton’s method solves equ for the new acceleration. The same function objects xnew and vnew then evaluate the new position and velocity:

NewtonSolver (equ, a);
xnew -> evaluate (a, x);
vnew -> evaluate (a, v);

xold->set(x);
vold->set(v);
aold->set(a);

21.3. The Störmer-Verlet method#

An explicit alternative is the Störmer-Verlet method:

\[\begin{split} \begin{array}{rcl} v_{n+1/2} & = & v_n + \frac{\tau}{2} a(x_n) \\ x_{n+1} & = & x_n + \tau \, v_{n+1/2} \\ v_{n+1} & = & v_{n+1/2} + \frac{\tau}{2} a(x_{n+1}) \end{array} \end{split}\]

with \(a(x) = M^{-1} F(x)\). In the notation of the generalized-\(\alpha\) method below, it is the Newmark family with \(\beta = 0\), \(\gamma = \frac{1}{2}\), i.e. \(x_{n+1} = x_n + \tau v_n + \frac{\tau^2}{2} a_n\), while the method above has \(\beta = \frac{1}{4}\), \(\gamma = \frac{1}{2}\). It needs one evaluation of the force per time-step, since \(a(x_{n+1})\) is reused in the next step. The method is of second order, symplectic, and time-reversible. Eliminating the velocities gives the two-step form

\[ x_{n+1} - 2 x_n + x_{n-1} = \tau^2 a(x_n), \]

which we use for the wave equation in Verlet time-stepping and Mass-lumping. As an explicit method it is only conditionally stable: for the oscillator \(\ddot x = -\omega^2 x\) one needs \(\tau \omega < 2\). Stiff springs therefore require small time-steps, and implicit methods are preferred.

21.4. Generalized-\(\alpha\) method#

Although the Newmark method preserves energy exactly for linear ODEs, it leads to instabilities for non-linear equations. For this reason, the following generalization is usually used in mechanical simulation codes:

By adding a bunch of parameters, one ends up with the Generalized \(\alpha\) method:

\[ M a_{n+1-\alpha_m} = F(x_{n+1-\alpha_f}) \]

with

\[\begin{split} \begin{array}{rcl} x_{n+1} & = & x_n + \tau v_n + \tau^2 ( ( \frac{1}{2} - \beta) a_n + \beta a_{n+1} ) \\ v_{n+1} & = & v_n + \tau ( (1-\gamma) a_n + \gamma a_{n+1} ) \\ x_{n+1-\alpha_f} & = & (1-\alpha_f) x_{n+1} + \alpha_f x_n \\ a_{n+1-\alpha_m} & = & (1-\alpha_m) a_{n+1} + \alpha_m a_n \end{array} \end{split}\]

Depending on one free parameter \(\rho^\infty\), parameters tuned for optimal accuracy and stability are obtained by

\[\begin{split} \begin{array}{rcl} \alpha_m & = & \frac{2 \rho^\infty - 1}{\rho^\infty + 1} \\ \alpha_f & = & \frac{\rho^\infty}{\rho^\infty + 1} \\ \beta & = & \frac{1}{4} (1 - \alpha_m + \alpha_f)^2 \\ \gamma & = & \frac{1}{2} - \alpha_m + \alpha_f \end{array} \end{split}\]

The parameter \(\rho^\infty \in [0,1]\) specifies the damping for high frequency functions: \(\rho^\infty = 1\) gives no numerical damping (like the Newmark method), smaller values damp high frequencies stronger. The implementation SolveODE_Alpha in ASC-ODE uses the variant \((1-\alpha_f) F(x_{n+1}) + \alpha_f F(x_n)\) instead of \(F(x_{n+1-\alpha_f})\), which coincides for linear \(F\). The reference is J. Chung and G.M. Hulbert A Time Integration Algorithm for Structural Dynamics With Improved Numerical Dissipation: The Generalized-α Method, J. Appl. Mech., 1993, see

An easy to read derivation is here.

21.5. Systems with constraints#

Think of a pendulum, where a mass \(m\) is attached to a (mass-less) stick of length \(l\). One can describe the system by the angle \(\alpha\) between the stick and the vertical axis. By some high-school mathematics, one splits the total gravitational force into the direction of the stick, and the orthogonal complement, and ends up with the equation for the angle \(\alpha\):

\[ \ddot \alpha = -\frac{g}{l} \sin \alpha \]

However, such a direct modeling with angles (so called generalized coordinates) becomes difficult for more complex systems. A simpler possibility is to pose the length constraint

\[ 0 = c(x) := | x - x_0 |^2 - l^2, \]

where \(x_0\) is the anchor point of the pendulum, and define the Lagrange function

\[ L(x, \lambda) = -U(x) + \left< \lambda , c(x) \right>, \]

with \(U(x) = m g x_z\), and pose the equation

\[\begin{split} \begin{array}{rcl} m_i \ddot x_i & = & \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} L(x, \lambda) \\ 0 & = & \nabla_\lambda L(x, \lambda) \end{array} \end{split}\]

The physical meaning of the Lagrange parameter \(\lambda\) is the longitudinal force in the pendulum (precisely: \(\lambda \nabla_x c\) is the force vector). This is an ordinary differential equation, with algebraic constraints, a so called differential-algebraic equation (DAE).

One can directly use the generalized-\(\alpha\) method for this DAE. The right-hand side is the gradient of the Lagrange function, the mass matrix on the left-hand side is extended by \(0\) for the Lagrange parameters:

\[\begin{split} \left( \begin{array}{cc} M & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{array} \right) \left( \begin{array}{c} \ddot x \\ 0 \end{array} \right) = \nabla L (x, \lambda) \end{split}\]

The Lagrange parameter is treated like an additional position variable. Its “acceleration” is an auxiliary unknown, only the resulting \(\lambda_{n+1}\) has a meaning. With the variant used in ASC-ODE, the rows of the Lagrange parameters enforce

\[ (1-\alpha_f) \, c(x_{n+1}) + \alpha_f \, c(x_n) = 0, \]

thus the constraint is satisfied exactly in every time-step, provided it is satisfied initially.

Some practical consequences of the missing equation for \(\dot \lambda\):

  • Explicit methods cannot be applied directly.

  • The initial values must be consistent: \(c(x(0)) = 0\), and also the time derivative \(\nabla c(x(0)) \cdot \dot x(0) = 0\), i.e. the initial velocity is tangential to the constraint.

  • The Lagrange parameter is sensitive to perturbations. Without numerical damping (\(\rho^\infty = 1\)), \(\lambda\) oscillates from time-step to time-step, while the positions are fine. Numerical damping with \(\rho^\infty < 1\) removes these oscillations.

21.6. Implementing mass-spring systems#

Merge branch Newmark from ASC-ODE into your main branch, see here.

For small systems it is reasonable to implement the right-hand-side function directly. For larger mechanical systems we describe the system by data structures, which are implemented in mechsystem/mass_spring.hpp. They are templated on the spatial dimension D:

template <int D> class Mass { public: double mass; Vec<D> pos; Vec<D> vel = 0.0; Vec<D> acc = 0.0; };
template <int D> class Fix  { public: Vec<D> pos; };

class Connector { public: enum CONTYPE { FIX=1, MASS=2 }; CONTYPE type; size_t nr; };
class Spring { public: double length; double stiffness; std::array<Connector,2> connectors; };

A Connector refers to either a fixed point or a mass, by its type and index. The class MassSpringSystem<D> has containers for fixes, masses and springs, and the gravity vector. The functions addFix and addMass return connectors, which are then used to add springs.

The state vector of the ODE consists of the positions of all masses, \(D\) values per mass. The class MSS_Function<D> evaluates the right-hand side for the MassSpringSystem. It interprets the state vector as a matrix with one row per mass (via asMatrix), starts with the gravitational forces, and adds the forces of all springs:

for (auto spring : mss.springs())
  {
    auto [c1,c2] = spring.connectors;
    Vec<D> p1, p2;
    if (c1.type == Connector::FIX)
      p1 = mss.fixes()[c1.nr].pos;
    else
      p1 = xmat.row(c1.nr);
    if (c2.type == Connector::FIX)
      p2 = mss.fixes()[c2.nr].pos;
    else
      p2 = xmat.row(c2.nr);

    double force = spring.stiffness * (norm(p1-p2)-spring.length);
    Vec<D> dir12 = 1.0/norm(p1-p2) * (p2-p1);
    if (c1.type == Connector::MASS)
      fmat.row(c1.nr) += force*dir12;
    if (c2.type == Connector::MASS)
      fmat.row(c2.nr) -= force*dir12;
  }

Finally every row is divided by its mass. Thus MSS_Function returns the acceleration \(M^{-1} F(x)\), and the mass function passed to the time-stepping method is the IdentityFunction. The derivative is computed by numerical differentiation.

Python bindings in bind_mass_spring.cpp allow to set up the system from Python. The function simulate(tend, steps) calls the generalized-\(\alpha\) method with \(\rho^\infty = 0.8\), and stores the new state in the masses:

from mass_spring import *

mss = MassSpringSystem3d()
mss.gravity = (0,0,-9.81)

mA = mss.add (Mass(1, (1,0,0)))
mB = mss.add (Mass(2, (2,0,0)))
f1 = mss.add (Fix( (0,0,0)) )
mss.add (Spring(1, 10, (f1, mA)))
mss.add (Spring(1, 20, (mA, mB)))

mss.simulate (0.1, 10)
print ("state = ", mss.getState())

The directory mechsystem contains

  • file mass_spring.cpp for testing a system from C++

  • file test_mass_spring.py for testing the system from Python

  • Jupyter-notebook file mass_spring.ipynb for testing the system including 3D visualization

21.7. Extending the code#

The following extensions are your own implementation, here are suggestions for the interfaces.

Distance constraints. A constraint fixes the distance between two masses, or between a mass and a fixed point:

class DistanceConstraint { public: double length; std::array<Connector,2> connectors; };

The MassSpringSystem gets a container of constraints and a function addConstraint. The state vector is extended by one Lagrange parameter per constraint, its dimension becomes \(D \cdot \#\text{masses} + \#\text{constraints}\). For the mass rows, MSS_Function adds the constraint forces \(\lambda \nabla_x c\) (divided by the mass, as all forces), for the constraint rows it returns the constraint \(c(x)\). The mass function is not the identity anymore, but must vanish for the Lagrange parameters. This is the existing Projector:

auto mass = std::make_shared<Projector> (dim, 0, D*mss.masses().size());

Exact derivative. Every spring contributes \(D \times D\) blocks to four positions of the Jacobi matrix of MSS_Function. Test your implementation by comparing with the numerical derivative.

Störmer-Verlet. A function with the same arguments as SolveODE_Newmark, but without the mass function:

void SolveODE_Verlet (double tend, int steps,
                      VectorView<double> x, VectorView<double> dx,
                      std::shared_ptr<NonlinearFunction> rhs,
                      std::function<void(double,VectorView<double>)> callback = nullptr);

Here, rhs returns the acceleration \(M^{-1} F(x)\), as MSS_Function does. As an explicit method, it needs neither Newton’s method nor the derivative of rhs. Alternatively, design a class hierarchy of time-steppers for second order systems, with a virtual function doStep(tau, x, v). Where do you store the acceleration \(a(x_{n+1})\), which is reused in the next time-step?

21.8. Exercises#

21.8.1. Mass-spring system#

  • build and run the examples in the mechsystem directory

  • add distance constraints to the MassSpring system. Enforce them using Lagrange multipliers.

  • the class MSS_Function implements the derivative using numerical differentiation. Implement the exact derivative.

  • Experiment with different mechanical structures:

    • Extend the double-pendulum to a chain

    • Build more complex structures using beams (for example a crane), and simulate vibration

    • Replace the springs of the double-pendulum by distance-constraints

    • Build a spinning top (German: Kreisel). We know angular momentum keeps it spinning. Connect three masses by distance constraints modeling the spinning top. Start with velocities consistent to a rotating motion

21.8.2. Energy and constraints#

  • Simulate the pendulum \(\ddot \alpha = -\frac{g}{l} \sin \alpha\) over a long time (e.g. 1000 periods) with the explicit Euler, implicit Euler, Crank-Nicolson, and Störmer-Verlet methods. Plot the energy over time.

  • Implement the Störmer-Verlet method for the MassSpringSystem. Find the largest stable time-step for a chain of masses and springs, and compare with the eigenvalues of the linearized system.

  • Simulate the pendulum as DAE with the generalized-\(\alpha\) method for \(\rho^\infty = 1\) and \(\rho^\infty = 0.8\). Plot the constraint \(c(x)\) and the Lagrange parameter \(\lambda\) over time.
